# Notes from the discussion of 18 October

A record of one long session between John and Claude: a review of
`design/cell_method.md`, a detour into more complicated designs that John
stopped, the build and full run of the note as written (step 31), a long
working-through of how the algorithm actually works, and the checks on the
results. Decisions John made are marked **John:**. Results are in
`31_RESULTS.md`; jobs in `31_STATUS.md`.

## 1. The review of the note

John asked for a deep conceptual review before implementing the note with
subagents. Four concerns were raised:

1. **The note understates what the method assumes.** Assumption 5 says
   that among households alike on everything the model sees, those that
   failed to report are like those that reported, in net worth. The
   algorithm assumes more: within a cell and size range, the chance that a
   holder says "none" depends on nothing else on the record (not net
   worth, not age, not whether it reported dividends). The scaling step
   (one multiplier per cell and range, applied to the odds) is the exact
   Bayes adjustment under that stronger assumption, and only under it. The
   example given: if people who skip their 1099s skip small interest and
   small dividends together, hidden holders should be concentrated among
   households reporting nothing, while the method spreads them toward
   households that reported other items (in step 30, 26% of hidden
   interest holders reported dividends, against 1.5% of remaining zeros).
2. **A free check.** The PUF gives, within each cell, the share holding
   each combination of the three items. The method targets each item on
   its own. Comparing combinations would test concern 1.
3. **The model may lock in its own fill-ins.** From pass 4 on it trains
   on the previous pass's fill-ins; a model able to recognise individual
   households could keep picking the same ones. Suggested checks: how many
   of one pass's hidden holders are picked again the next pass, and the
   model's chances for trained households against the 2% held back.
4. **Some zeros are not answers.** The crude non-filers carry CPS-reported
   interest (which under-reports too) on the PUF side of the cell table;
   split couples get interest divided by each partner's share of earnings,
   so a partner with no earnings gets zero by construction; split-off
   adults get interest from a yes/no flag and one lump amount.

Smaller points: whether the wage cut-offs of the cells stretch for other
waves; the married over-$200,000 cell (74% of interest zeros filled in
step 30, and the largest SCF excess); capital loss carryovers in PUF line
13 that an SCF respondent would not report; "300 households" ambiguous;
three wording slips in the worked example.

**John:** does not care about concern 4; does care about 1 to 3.

## 2. The detour John stopped

Asked what would fix concern 1, Claude proposed, in turn:

1. splitting each item's cells by whether the household holds the other
   items, so the PUF sets those counts too;
2. a table of combinations (none, interest only, dividends only, both)
   with targets from the PUF and the rule that a household can only gain
   items;
3. because combinations double with each new variable, a list of targets
   (single items by range, pairs of items) met by drawing candidate
   fill-ins from the model and tilting them, as the post-training tilt
   does.

**John:** "this is way too overengineered … the whole goal is to put X on
the same terms in the SCF (or any donor survey) as X in the PUF when
learning Y|X. that's it. does that require this?" Claude agreed it did
not: the combinations question is empirical and could be checked first.

John then asked for a generic method covering all the reasons X differs
between files. Claude sorted the reasons (definition differences, fixed by
a set conversion; under-reporting; different people in the file, which
belongs to the population work) and proposed rank matching within cells:
order the survey's households, put zero-reporters in order of the model's
chance, give each the PUF's amount at the same position.

- That put every zero-reporter below every reporter. **John:** "that seems
  horrible." A household with $1,500 of hidden interest would land below
  one reporting $50.
- The patch, letting the model's draw place zero-reporters anywhere, needs
  a rule for how the chance of reporting varies with the amount. **John:**
  "we are close to reinventing the S curve stuff from before."

Where it ended: the note's method already is the generic method. Its
settings sheet covers definition differences (the concept conversion),
wrong amounts (the dollar rule) and hidden holders (counts by cell and size
range read off the PUF, with no curve fitted). That is why per-cell counts
beat fitted curves in step 29. **John:** "no just build the note thing.
and run it. fully."

The lesson recorded for later sessions: check whether a problem is real
and how big before adding machinery to fix it.

## 3. Building and running it

Built from step 29's per-cell version (`29_train.py`, rule C4), changed to
the note: settings read from a sheet, the cell table by broad size ranges
with the top line, one multiplier per wave, cell and range. A 4-pass test
run first, then three seeds of 80 passes, the files, reweighting on two
count bases, the comparisons, and a report. Details in `31_STATUS.md` and
`31_RESULTS.md`. The cell table reproduces the note's worked example to
within the effect of slices that straddle a range edge.

## 4. How the algorithm works, as it was finally understood

John found the first explanations unclear and said so repeatedly. What did
not work: layering detail (passes, odds, the assumption stated three ways)
on top of the idea, and loose words such as "the model" without saying
which. What worked: a small example by hand, questions answered one at a
time, and John's own summary.

### The data

Each SCF record keeps two versions of each mapped item (interest,
dividends, capital gains):

- the **reported value**, kept aside and never changed, used only to know
  who said zero;
- the **training value**, the one the model sees. For reporters it always
  equals the reported value. For zero-reporters it is redrawn every pass
  from pass 3.

PUF records never change. Example:

| | Net worth | Dividends (reported) | Interest (reported) | Interest (training value), passes 1–2 | Pass 3 | Pass 4 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Household A | $900,000 | $3,000 | $0 | $0 | $50 | $80 |
| Household B | $15,000 | $0 | $0 | $0 | $0 | $0 |
| Household C | $300,000 | $0 | $250 | $250 | $250 | $250 |

### The model

There is one model: the transformer of `02_model.py`. It is trained, used
to pick hidden holders during training, and used afterwards to make the
file. Training repeats one exercise: take a record, blank some variables,
give chances for each possible value (84 dollar slices per variable),
score by the chance given to the value in the slot, adjust. So its chances
for "interest, given a record like this" settle near the share of training
records like this with each amount.

### Where a zero-reporter's chance of holding comes from

For household A, blank interest and ask the model for its chances, under
the SCF label, given everything else on the record. Its chance of holding
is 100% minus its chance of $0, say 40%: among SCF records that look like
A, about 40% have interest. The model never sees A's own answer.

### John's summary, confirmed

"For each non-reporter, we estimate the probability that they have
interest, based on their observables. That gives us a sense of who is more
likely … and by how much. Then we just normalize that to fit the targets
supplied by the PUF." Yes.

### The by-hand version

One cell, 100 SCF households:

| | Households | Reported interest | Said zero |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rich | 40 | 16 | 24 |
| Poor | 60 | 4 | 56 |

1. The PUF says 40 of 100 hold interest; 20 reported; 20 are hiding.
2. Holders report at 20 / 40 = 50%.
3. Assumed: rich and poor holders both report at that 50%.
4. Rich: 16 reported, so 32 holders, 16 hidden among 24 zeros (67%).
   Poor: 4 reported, so 8 holders, 4 hidden among 56 zeros (7%).
5. Each rich zero-reporter is filled in with chance 67%, each poor one 7%.

The real version differs only in that households are described by many
variables at once, so a model replaces the table.

### The two chances

The confusion that took longest: two different chances.

- **Chance of holding** varies freely within a cell; the model learns it.
- **Chance that a holder reports** is assumed the same for everyone in a
  cell and size range.

The data alone cannot separate them: a group that reports less might hold
less or hide more. The assumption decides it. If rich holders always
reported and poor ones hid, the method would pick the wrong households.

### Other mapped variables

The guess for interest uses dividends and capital gains at their current
filled-in values, not as reported. The three items are filled in turn, in
a random order each pass, each using the others' latest values; that is
part of why the process repeats. Still assumed: whether a household hides
interest does not depend on whether it hides dividends. **John:** "that's
fine because this is an assumption we'd be making anyway."

### Why passes 1 and 2 train on the SCF as reported

So the model has a first estimate of which kinds of households tend to
have interest before it is asked to guess; an untrained model's guesses
would be random. Starting at pass 3 is carried over from steps 22 to 29
and was never tested. **John:** mark it as a possible thing to look into
(one seed each starting at passes 2, 3, 6 and 10); not run.

### Why repeat, and what the loss is scored against

The repeated passes are a fixed-point iteration: fill in from the current
model, train on the filled-in data, fill in again, until the model and the
fill-ins agree. In statistics this is data augmentation (Tanner and Wong,
1987), multiple imputation inside the estimation. For a zero-reporter the
model is scored against this pass's fill-in, a draw from its own chances
scaled to the PUF's counts; everything else (PUF records, SCF reporters,
net worth, wages) is scored against real values. The passes add no new
information: who is filled in comes only from how SCF reporters differ from
zero-reporters, plus the assumption. The loop is the calculation.

Why draw at random rather than take the most likely: taking the top 3
every pass would make "no interest" mean "poor" more sharply than is true.

## 5. Filling in during training against filling in once

Asked whether "train, fill in once, retrain" is equivalent in expectation:
no. A model trained on the SCF as reported gives the chance of reporting;
scaling that by one factor per cell and range orders households correctly
but spreads the fill-ins too evenly. Example, one cell, holders of under
$100 reporting at 30%:

| | True chance of holding | Right chance of being a hidden holder, given they said zero | Fill-in-once, reporting chance scaled to the same total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Household 1 | 80% | 74% | 63% |
| Household 2 | 20% | 15% | 26% |

The fill-in-once version can be corrected exactly by dividing by each cell
and range's reporting rate from the cell table. It is simpler and cannot
lock in its own fill-ins, but fills the three items nearly independently
and has never been tested. Not run; offered as a comparison.

## 6. The results and how to judge them

Results in `31_RESULTS.md`. The criteria discussed, in order of strength:
mechanics (targets hit, stable shares, no extra dollars), untargeted
comparisons with outside data, the fake survey (the only test of who), and
stability to the choices made.

- **Dropped as a test:** holders by AGI bracket against IRS tables. The
  file's income items are drawn under the PUF label and the reweighting
  targets them, so they say nothing about the fill-in.
- **The combinations check** found about 3 points too many units holding
  none of the three items and too few holding dividends without interest.
  Claude first called this "the opposite" of concern 1; that was wrong and
  was corrected: it is concern 1. Households that report one item tend to
  report the others (interest reported by about 73% of dividend reporters
  who hold it, against about 35% of the rest), and the method reads that
  as a link in holding.
- **The fake survey** came back without surprises: the right share of
  hidden holders, dollars close to the truth, and most of the error of
  taking zeros as zeros removed.

**John:** "barring some disaster in the fake survey this is all good news";
there was none.

## 8. Also recorded

  the best evidence of who holds interest; the minimal model sees only
  total net worth. John raised this; worth adding later.
- The training weights: each record's survey weight, rescaled so the SCF
  as a whole carries weight equal to its number of records; each wave
  carries roughly equal weight. The cell table uses the raw 2016 weights.
- Total net worth before reweighting was first reported as within 1% of
  the SCF; across seeds it ranges from $92 trillion to $102 trillion, so
  that closeness is partly luck (corrected in `31_RESULTS.md`).
