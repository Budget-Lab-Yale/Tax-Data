# Where things stand, late on 14 October

Written at the end of the first day of the minimal example, after a long
session with John. Read this first; the numbered reports hold the numbers.

## What exists

A fresh seven-variable build in this folder, every step reviewed by John:

- `01` the stacked table: 2015 PUF plus 2016 SCF, seven variables (married,
  dependents, wages, taxable interest, dividends, capital gains, plus
  other_agi owned by the PUF and net worth owned by the SCF), 84 rank bands
  per amount within source and year. SCF split-off units dropped. A
  three-wave version (2016, 2019, 2022 SCF) is also built, for the years
  step.
- `02` the model: masked any-subset transformer, four layers, width 128,
  about 600,000 parameters, three minutes on one GPU. The size sweep
  (`02_SIZE_SWEEP.md`) says this size is already on the flat part of the
  curve at seven variables; bigger only memorizes.
- `03` generation in block order (own-survey variables first, borrowed
  ones after, demographics last), plus exact oversampling of the top by
  drawing one variable first from a boosted distribution and weighting by
  p/q.
- `04` the rank-dollar map (full sorted list of real values per band) and a
  crude 2019 map (one SOI growth factor per amount).
- `05` the tilt: entropy calibration on the generated sample, exact or
  soft targets. 136 IRS 2019 targets by AGI bracket cost 0.007 nats on the
  generated file and 0.006 on the real PUF. Untargeted relationships move
  under about two points.
- `06`, `07` wealth tables and a first baseline on the old stack, kept as a
  record.

## What is wrong

Net worth generated for PUF-style records is about double the SCF's, at
every wage and dividend level (`06_WEALTH_TABLES.md`). The cause is the
holder-share gap the plan's D23 already described: 27 percent of PUF
returns have taxable interest against 12 percent of SCF units, because
survey respondents report small amounts as zero. Ranks within each source
take those zeros at face value, so the same band means a richer household
in the SCF, and "has a little interest" is learned as "rich". Production's
wealth imputation hit the same thing and zeroes PUF amounts under $100
(`src/imputations/wealth.R`). The minimal example skipped the translation
step because it lived in experiment code we chose not to reuse.

Drawing net worth under the SCF label (the owner of the variable) fixes the
top but not the middle; hiding other_agi changes nothing; the problem is
visible in band space before any dollars exist. So it is the training
data's meaning, not the dollar step and not the label.

## The fix chosen: coarsened zeros (one-stage)

An SCF zero for interest, dividends or capital gains means "zero or small",
not "zero". Training is changed in the scoring rule only: for those rows
and variables the model is scored on the total probability it puts on the
set {exact zero, bands up to a dollar line}, instead of on zero alone. The
split between zero and small is then learned from the PUF, where it is
observed, and reaches SCF households through the shared variables. No
imputation, no choice of "most likely" households, uncertainty kept. This
is the standard likelihood for coarsened data; the transformer's
contribution is that one model holds both surveys so the PUF's knowledge
is available on SCF rows. The two-stage translation of D23 (flip the most
likely non-holders, then rank) stays as the fallback.

The dollar line: production used $100, chosen because it is the floor at
which the PUF's holder share matches the SCF's (29.8% -> 20.4% against the
SCF's 19.4% for interest plus dividends). The rule for picking it per
variable, and for deciding which variables need it, is in the next
section.

## Rule for the dollar line and for which variables

For each variable shared by a donor survey and the PUF:

1. Compare the holder shares (share with a nonzero amount), weighted,
   same year.
2. If the donor's share is lower, assume the donor censors small amounts.
   Set the line f per variable as the smallest dollar amount such that
   the PUF's share with amount above f equals the donor's holder share.
   Check it on the amounts: above f the donor's and PUF's distributions of
   positive amounts (by rank among holders) should agree in level; below
   f the donor should be short.
3. If the donor's share is higher, censoring is not the explanation; it is
   a definition or population difference, and it is flagged for a
   translation of the concept, not a coarsening.
4. If the shares agree within noise (wages: about 70% in both), do nothing.

The line is chosen from the data, not by hand, and recorded per variable
and per wave in the table's metadata.

- Income groups are never a model input; they are tilt targets on derived

  the next round.
- Partly-missing variables (PUF age) stay out until their missingness is
  understood.

## Added after midnight: the hard-line fix failed

The coarsened-zero retrain (model v3) ran. PUF-style holder shares still
match the PUF, but SCF-style records came out with 98% holding investment
income and SCF-style wealth far too high; PUF-style wealth did not improve.
Reason: the source label lets the model learn the SCF separately, and with
no SCF row pinning "exact zero" any more, "small" was an equally good
answer under the SCF label. The PUF's knowledge of the zero/small split
never crossed over because nothing forced it to. The check on the dollar
lines also showed that SCF holders above the line report about twice the
PUF's amounts at the median: the SCF drops holders at every level, more
often the smaller the amount, not only below a line.

So the fix cannot live in the loss alone. The principled version has
three parts that go together, for John to decide on:

1. Predict shared variables (married, n_dep, wages, interest, dividends,
   capital_gains) without the source token, so one rule serves both
   surveys and the PUF is the authority; owned variables keep the label.
   This is the plan's "label-indifferent for shared variables", enforced
   rather than hoped for.
2. A reporting curve instead of a hard line: the probability an SCF
   amount is reported, rising with its size, estimated from the two
   surveys and used in the loss (an SCF zero is scored on
   p(0) + sum_b p(b)(1 - g(b)); a reported band b on p(b) g(b)).
3. Shared variables banded on one dollar scale (the PUF's cut points per
   year) so a band means the same dollars under both labels; a reporting
   curve needs that. John's earlier worry, that SCF dollars may be off, is
   testable: if a curve g <= 1 explains both the SCF's holder share and its
   reported amounts, the dollars are fine.

and its files are under model/v3fix/ and gen/v3fix/. The code in this
folder is the v3 version (coarsened loss, net worth under the SCF label);

## The problem, stated abstractly (15 October, 12:30 a.m.)

We have two datasets that describe the same kind of unit. Dataset P has
variables X and Z. Dataset S has variables X and Y. We want one model
that can produce records with X, Y and Z together.

The model learns Y from S, Z from P, and the link between them through X,
which both have. That only works if X means the same thing in both
datasets. If it does not, the model learns Y given S's version of X and
applies it to P's version, and Y comes out wrong.

Ours do not mean the same thing. P (the PUF) records X exactly. S (the
SCF) records a noisy, incomplete version of X: small values missing, some
holders dropped, some amounts rounded. So "X = $300" in S describes a
different kind of household than "X = $300" in P.

That is the whole problem: the bridge variable is measured differently in
the two datasets it is supposed to bridge.

There are only three places to fix it:

1. **Fix the data.** Change S's X, or P's X, or both, so they mean the
   same thing before the model sees them. Production did this (the $100
   threshold on PUF interest and dividends). Translation (D23) is this. The
   coarsest-common-resolution split (below) is this.
2. **Fix the model.** Tell the model that S's X is a noisy reading of the
   true X, and let it learn the truth from P. The coarsened loss was an
   attempt at this. It failed because the model was free to keep the two
   datasets separate through the source label.
3. **Fix the output.** Let the model be wrong and correct it afterward
   with targets. The tilt does this. It works, but it distorts Y given X to
   get the Y totals right, and the size of the correction (the move in
   nats) tells you how wrong the model was.

Everything discussed on 14 and 15 October is one of those three.

### The candidate rule under option 1: coarsest common resolution

Every shared variable is stored at the coarsest resolution any source
measures it reliably; finer detail becomes a variable owned by the source
that has it. For interest: the shared slot is "interest at or above the
survey's detection limit, else zero" (both sources; "has it" then means
the same thing in both); "interest below the limit" is PUF-owned, absent
on SCF rows, like other_agi. Production's $100 threshold is the first
half of this; the second half exists because we generate the PUF and must
produce the small amounts too.

What makes it general rather than hand-crafted:
- The detection limit is estimated per variable and per donor from the
  comparison with the authority (the reporting rate by amount, where it
  reaches about one), not picked by hand. `01_DETECTION_TABLE.md` is the
  first such table.
- The same rule covers brackets, rounding and topcoding.
- Which variables need it comes out of the comparison (wages do not; the
  three investment items do).
- How coarse: coarsen until the shared slot's distribution given the other
  shared variables is the same in both sources within noise. This is the
  label-indifference test used as a design tool.

Assumptions it makes: the authority is the truth for the variable; whether
a unit reports depends on the amount and not on wealth given the amount;
above the limit the donor's amounts are about right; the limit is a dollar
amount. Each has a test (raise the limit; compare reporters above the
limit with authority holders on the other shared variables; the detection
table's within-bracket means). The backstop for all of them is the move
the tilt needs to hit the donor's own tables.

When the donor is HIGHER (the SCF's pass-through income): that is a
different concept, not detection. The rule says coarsen to whatever the
two agree on (perhaps only "has any"), and make the amounts source-owned
on both sides, or translate the concept by hand (D23).

Ranks: within-source ranks stay for both slots. Among holders above the
limit, once the holder shares agree, the same rank is close to the same
dollars; the exact version puts the shared slot on a common dollar scale.
