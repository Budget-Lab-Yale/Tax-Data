# Notes from the discussion of 15 October, evening

What John and Claude worked out while the v4 and v4b runs were going.
The decisions that came out of it are in `design/plan.md` (decisions 16
and 17, the open item on a model-side answer to translation, the future
refinements section, the stopping point). This file keeps the reasoning.

## 1. The pipeline, stated for colleagues

1. **Pretraining.** One transformer trained on every source stacked
   together, each record carrying its source and year. It learns to fill
   in any variable given any others. The result is one probability model
   that can be asked any conditional distribution.
2. **The prior for a year.** Everything chosen before looking at that
   year's targets:
   - which years' surveys to borrow from (the year mix, section 2);
   - how positions on the PUF's scale turn into that year's dollars (the
     2015 PUF cut points grown by the IRS growth in each item; by bracket
     later, see the plan's future refinements);
   - how borrowed variables are drawn (section 3).
   With one PUF, the PUF variables are always generated with the 2015
   label (decision 6).
3. **Post-training.** Draw a large file from the prior and move it as
   little as possible to hit the year's targets (section 4). How far it
   has to move grades the prior.

## 2. The year prior

**How it works.** The year is a category the model knows only for years
in its training data. To build a year's file, each record's year token is
drawn from a mix of nearby training years; for 2019, with waves for income
years 2015, 2018 and 2021, say 60% 2018, 20% each of the others. That is
the same as sampling from a weighted average of what each wave says. The
plan calls it the rolling prior (decision 3). Not yet built: every run so
far used one year for every record.

**Choosing it is not arbitrary, even though many mixes would hit the
targets after the tilt.** The tilt fixes what is targeted; the prior
decides everything else. So the mix must be judged on what is not
targeted. Three ways, each giving a number:

1. **Leave one wave out.** Build the prior from the other waves and score
   how well it predicts the left-out wave's records (average
   log-probability). Repeat for each wave; choose the weights, or the
   width of the window around the target year, that predict best. This is
   cross-validation, as in choosing a smoothing bandwidth. With three waves
   it is noisy; the SCF goes back to 1989 (twelve waves).
2. **Fit the weights directly** to the left-out wave ("stacking" of
   predictive distributions: Yao, Vehtari, Simpson and Gelman, 2018).
3. **Hold out some targets.** Tilt to most of a year's targets and score
   the prior on the rest. Choosing the prior with the smallest tilt is
   tempting, but it can favour a prior good on the targeted moments and
   bad elsewhere.

Two parts can be learned more directly. The dollar part from SOI's tables
by AGI bracket, published every year. The year part inside the model, by
making year a number instead of a category so that the model learns drift
and can be asked about 2019 directly; the risk is extrapolation past the
last wave.

Plan item: "Next tests", item 6 (rolling prior bandwidth), now with these
three methods.

## 3. Borrowed variables: draw them as the owner would see the record

**The finding** (`16_MODEL_SIDE_FIXES.md`). The model reads whatever slots
a record has. A generated PUF record has other income filled in; SCF
records never do. Until v4b, net worth was drawn on PUF records either
under the PUF label, or under the SCF label with other income visible.
Both ask about combinations no training record had.

**Why the PUF-label version works at all.** A regression analogue: fit
wealth = a + b·wages + c·interest + d·(PUF label) + e·(other income) on SCF
rows only. On those rows the PUF label is always 0 and other income always
missing, so d and e cannot be estimated. A network has no "cannot be
estimated": it answers anyway, and the unidentified parts are set by its
structure and starting point, not by data. The answer came out about right
for E4 because the translation rule made the shared variables mean the same
dollars in both files, so the network learned that the label matters little
for them and kept it that way for wealth. E7 to E9, trained on the same
data, set the unidentified parts differently, and their wealth jumped when
other income was visible. Same data, different answers: the data were not
deciding.

**What the network might carry over, and why that is unsafe.** If every
variable were 20% higher under the PUF label, the network might also raise
wealth when the label is switched to the PUF. Whether that is right depends
on why the PUF is higher. If the SCF under-reports income, wealth should
not be raised; if the PUF holds richer people at the same reported income,
perhaps it should. The network sees the same pattern either way and cannot
tell. Only outside knowledge can (documentation, IRS totals, linked
records).

**The rule** (decision 16): draw a borrowed variable under its owner's
label with the slots the owner never has left out, and with a year token
the owner has. The model is then asked only kinds of questions it saw in
training. Leaving other income out imposes the standard statistical
matching assumption (given the shared variables, wealth and other income
are unrelated) explicitly, instead of leaving it to the network. If wealth
does depend on other income (pensions, business income), the fix is to
give the SCF those variables in tax terms so that some records have both.

**What changed** (v4 against v4b): high earners' wealth moved most of the
way to the SCF (mean $3.37 million to $4.04 million, SCF $4.50 million);
the middle became a little too rich; returns with no wages did not change.

## 4. What the tilt is

It is reweighting: the generated records get weights, the network is not
changed. But the weights follow a formula, exp(λ·f(record)), where f are
the record's values on the targets and λ the numbers the tilt solves for.
So in principle the tilt defines a new model, the pretrained model times
that formula. **Correction (same evening, `18_DISTILL.md`):** in practice
the formula does not carry over to fresh draws. Several targets are dollar
amounts, so the factor is exponential in dollars, and on new records with
amounts more extreme than any in the tilted file a handful of records take
nearly all the weight (effective sample between 0.0003% and 46% of each
new file of 400,000). The weights belong to the file they were solved on;
a model that can be sampled afresh needs distillation.
It is the minimum relative-entropy change that hits the targets, the same
mathematics as entropy calibration in survey weighting.

The network itself can also be changed to generate the tilted
distribution, by training it on the tilted file (distillation) or by
adjusting its parameters to hit the targets at the smallest move (direct
gradient). What that would buy: one network per year that draws
equal-weight records directly; robustness when a tilt is large and
reweighting leaves a few records carrying most of the weight; cleaner
chaining from year to year (decision 11); and a literal version of the
post-training analogy. What it costs: a training run per year, an
approximate rather than exact hit, more to verify. At a move of 0.024 nats
it buys no accuracy. Both were tried (`18_DISTILL.md`, `19_DIRECT.md`).
Distillation result: the fine-tuned network gets most of the way (89 of 136
IRS targets within 2 standard errors, against 32 before post-training) and
needs a final reweight of about 0.005 nats; fine-tune plus reweight moves
about 0.033 nats against 0.024 for the tilt alone, because the fine-tune
also shifts things no target asks for (dependents most clearly).
Direct adjustment (`19_DIRECT.md`): 112 to 113 of 136 targets within 2
standard errors, a move of 0.026 to 0.033 nats, misses concentrated in
returns with no or very low AGI, 14 to 22 GPU minutes against seconds for
the tilt, and the result depends on the fill order. Reweighting stays the
method for a single year (decision 8); distillation is the route if a
model that can be sampled is needed.

**Rank targets become dollar lines** (decision 17). "The top 1% holds 38%"
depends on the weights through who is in the top 1%, so it had to be solved
in a loop, which never settled. Converted to "N returns above the SCF's
99th percentile in dollars, holding $X", it is solved once.

## 5. Confidence intervals

The setup gives them more naturally than the current pipeline's chain of
imputations. Sources of uncertainty:

| Source | How to get it | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Drawing noise | Draw more records | Negligible |
| Training data | Train about 20 models on bootstrap resamples of the PUF and SCF (the SCF ships replicate weights), tilt each, compute the statistic on each; the spread is the interval. The logic of multiple imputation | About 2 GPU hours at the current size |
| Targets | Draw each replicate's targets from their sampling error | None extra |
| Assumptions (translation rule, year mix, dollar growth) | Not statistical; sensitivity runs reported separately | Per assumption |

Not started. Added to the plan as an open item.

## 6. Targets in v4b

- **Targeted, hit exactly:** 136 IRS targets for 2019. For each of 15 AGI
  brackets, the number of returns, the number with wages, interest,
  dividends and capital gains, and the dollar amount of each (135), plus
  total joint returns. AGI is computed from the drawn parts.
- **Targeted, within about 2%:** eight SCF wealth targets for 2019. Total
  net worth; returns above $1 million; at the SCF's 50th, 90th and 99th
  percentiles in dollars, the number of returns above the line and the net
  worth they hold. The two counts at the 90th percentile and at $1 million
  pull against each other (−1.7% and +1.7%).
- **Not targeted:** dependents; married except the joint total; other
  income alone; how items go together; wealth by income or wage group; the
  split of wealth inside the top 1%.
- **Untargeted and off:** returns with no wages too rich; the middle a
  little rich; the top 0.1% thin (13.2% against 14.7%); single parents
  too few.

A target at the SCF's 99.9th percentile would be noisy and biased low: the
SCF has a few dozen tax units above it and excludes the Forbes 400. The top
is its own item in the plan.

## 7. Nonfilers next, and why

The PUF is tax units selected on having enough income to file. The SCF is
everyone. Part of what looks wrong (no-wage returns too rich, the 30% of
SCF units with no wages against 17% of returns) is two populations compared
as one. A plain threshold rule applied to the SCF left 91 million units
against 158 million returns, because the SCF tax-unit file lacks Social
Security, pension, business and rental income and many people file below
the threshold. This needs the filing model of the CPS nonfiler work already
in this repository, not a threshold. A fair interim check of the no-wage
gap: no-wage returns split by investment income, model against SCF.

## 8. A model-side answer to translation

The translation rule is good enough for now, but John wants a mechanism in
the transformer itself. `16_MODEL_SIDE_FIXES.md` found that E9 (the
reporting curve in the loss) had no bug and, once other income was left
out of the net worth draw, came close to E4. It cannot do better than E4,
because net worth is still learned from what the SCF reported, so a
reported zero lumps true non-holders with holders who did not report. The
version that could do better treats the true amount as unknown inside the
model, fills it in during training from the model's own prediction and the
chance of not reporting, and makes net worth depend on the true amount. It
still rests on an assumption no data here can test: that a non-reporter is
as wealthy as a reporter with the same amount. Proposed as E15, not run.

## 9. The true-amount model, with a worked example (to discuss with John)

A made-up SCF of 100 households and one income item, interest. The
numbers are chosen to be easy, not estimated.

| Truth | Households | Interest | Mean wealth | How many report their interest |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| No interest | 60 | $0 | $100,000 | (nothing to report) |
| Small holders | 20 | $50 | $200,000 | 5 report $50; 15 report $0 |
| Large holders | 20 | $5,000 | $1,000,000 | all 20 |

What the SCF shows:

| Reported interest | Households | Mean wealth |
|---|---:|---:|
| $0 | 75 (60 true zeros and 15 hidden small holders) | $120,000 |
| $50 | 5 | $200,000 |
| $5,000 | 20 | $1,000,000 |

The PUF, for the same 100 tax units, shows the true amounts: 60 at $0, 20
at $50, 20 at $5,000.

**The current rule (E4)** gives each PUF record the wealth of SCF
households who reported the same amount:

| PUF interest | Returns | Wealth given | Truth | Error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $0 | 60 | $120,000 | $100,000 | +20% |
| $50 | 20 | $200,000 | $200,000 | none |
| $5,000 | 20 | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 | none |
| Total | 100 | $31.2 million | $30.0 million | +4% |

The positive amounts are right, because the 5 households that reported $50
are like the 15 that did not (assumption 1 below). The zeros are wrong,
because the SCF's $0 group contains the 15 hidden small holders, who are
richer than true non-holders.

**The true-amount model** treats each SCF household's true interest as
unknown when it reports $0. It works out:

1. *How many hidden holders there are.* The PUF has 20 holders of $50 per
   100 returns; the SCF shows 5. So 25% of small holders report, and 15
   of the SCF's 75 zeros are hidden holders. This is the reporting curve.
2. *Which households they are likely to be.* It cannot know for certain;
   it uses the other variables. A $0 household whose wealth and wages look
   like those of the reported $50 holders is more likely to be a hidden
   holder than one that looks like a typical non-holder.
3. *Wealth given the true amount.* With the hidden holders taken out, the
   true non-holders' mean wealth is $100,000, and a PUF return with no
   interest gets $100,000. Correct.

In practice the household is not assigned to one case: a household that
is 30% likely to be a hidden holder counts 30% as a holder and 70% as a
non-holder in training, and the fill-ins are recomputed as the model
improves (the EM algorithm, or multiple imputation inside training).

**What it assumes:**

1. *A hidden holder is as wealthy as a reporting holder with the same
   amount.* No data here can test this; linked SCF and IRS records could.
   If, say, richer households keep better records and report more often,
   the hidden holders are poorer than the reporters, and the model
   overstates their wealth.
2. *The reporting curve is right.* It comes from comparing holder shares
   in the PUF and the SCF, which assumes the two cover the same people.
   They do not: the SCF's extra nonfilers look like non-reporters and
   inflate the count of hidden holders (`16_MODEL_SIDE_FIXES.md` found the
   curve implies negative wealth for true non-holders among low earners).
   So this comes after the nonfiler work.

**Size in the real data** (`16_MODEL_SIDE_FIXES.md`, under the current
curve): SCF households reporting $0 interest have mean wealth $292,000;
true non-holders, $124,000. The effect on mean wealth of PUF-style records
is about 7 to 14% for each of interest, dividends and capital gains, with
overlap.

**Why it matters beyond wealth.** It is the general tool for a survey zero
that hides a real amount (CPS, CEX, SIPP), and the one thing a joint model
of the PUF and a survey can do that separate models cannot: it needs to
know that the PUF and the SCF are two readings of the same true amount.

**To discuss:** whether to build it (E15 in `16_MODEL_SIDE_FIXES.md`,
about half a day of code) after the nonfiler work, and whether linked
records or published validation studies of SCF reporting exist to check
assumption 1.
