# Survey answers next to tax and information-return amounts

This note collects outside evidence on how people's survey answers about income compare with the amounts on their own tax returns or information returns. It is meant as prior information for the rebuild's reporting rules: the rules that connect a survey's reported amount to the amount that would appear on a tax return. It was assembled by a web literature search on 2026-10-17. Each study is marked with how closely it was read.

## Summary of what is known

The strongest person-level evidence comes from the Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement (CPS ASEC) linked by the Census Bureau to IRS and Social Security Administration records. It tells a consistent story. Wages and Social Security benefits are reported well: survey totals come to roughly 96–100% of the administrative totals, and people who receive them almost always say so. Retirement-plan distributions (pensions and IRA withdrawals, measured from Form 1099-R) are reported badly, and almost all of the shortfall comes from recipients who report nothing at all, not from recipients who report too little. About half of the people with a 1099-R report no retirement income. The chance of reporting nothing falls steadily as the 1099-R amount rises, but stays near 30% even in the top fifth of amounts. IRA-only recipients report nothing about 80% of the time. Interest and dividends are under-reported in the same way but less is published on them at the person level. For people 65 and older, 49% report any interest or dividends, against 61% with some on tax returns, and the survey captures 63% of the tax-return dollars (77% for ages 18–64). Linked studies in Canada and Spain find 21–71% of tax-recorded recipients of interest, dividends or investment income reporting none. In Canada the share drops to about 10% when respondents answer from records. No study was found that matches individual Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) respondents' answers to their own tax returns and reports the result. The Federal Reserve does hold such a match for list-sample cases, but it is used only for sample design and nonresponse adjustment. Published SCF-to-IRS comparisons are comparisons of totals and of shares within income classes. They show the same pattern as the CPS: interest and dividends 10–46% below IRS totals depending on the year, and most households with interest-bearing assets reporting no interest income. Self-employment income goes the other way: people report more to surveys than to the IRS, and the IRS's own audit studies find more than half of sole-proprietor income missing from returns. The tax return is therefore not "truth" for low-visibility income, though it is close to truth for wages, interest, dividends and pensions, which are covered by information returns. The IRS estimates net misreporting of 1–5% for those items.

## Wages and salaries

**Who reports zero.** People with W-2 wages almost always report earnings. In the 2013 CPS ASEC, among people 65 and older, 21.6% report earnings against 25.1% with administrative earnings (Bee and Mitchell 2017, Appendix Table 6, Panel A). The gap is wider in this older group than one would expect for prime-age workers. The CPS totals match: 100.3% of administrative earnings for ages 18–64 and 98.1% for 65 and older (Table 1). No study read here reports the share of W-2 recipients reporting zero by W-2 amount. The older literature suggests small and irregular jobs are the ones left out (Marquis and others 1981, as summarized in Moore, Stinson and Welniak 2000).

**Dollar ratio among reporters.** Close to one, with substantial noise. Coder (1992), matching the 1990 SIPP to tax returns for married couples filing jointly, finds mean SIPP wages 4% below tax-return wages and a correlation of 0.83. This is as summarized by Moore, Stinson and Welniak (2000); the original was not read. In the CPS, the log correlation of survey and administrative earnings among people 65 and older with both is 0.72, or 0.85 when imputed survey values are dropped (Bee and Mitchell 2017, Appendix Table 6). Imboden, Voorheis and Weber (2023) find a mean log gap of −0.068 for wage earners (tax amount below survey amount), with a large spike at zero.

**Other characteristics.** Item nonresponse to the earnings question is highest at both the bottom and the top of true earnings (Bollinger and others 2019). This matters for how a survey imputes missing answers, which the rebuild handles separately from reporting. The classic record-check finding is that survey errors are negatively correlated with true earnings: people with low earnings tend to over-report and people with high earnings tend to under-report. That finding is not re-verified here from primary sources. The Swedish evidence below (Kapteyn and Ypma 2007) suggests part of it comes from errors in the administrative records and in the linkage itself.

## Interest

**Who reports zero.** Every source points to the same thing: many holders of small interest-bearing balances report no interest.
- CPS, ages 65 and older, interest and dividends combined: 49.0% report any against 61.4% with some on Form 1040. Dropping imputed survey values, it is 31.1% against 49.9% (Bee and Mitchell 2017, Appendix Table 6). The study does not report the zero-reporting rate by amount for interest.
- SCF 2001: of households with AGI under $50,000 that own interest-bearing assets, 75% report no interest income. The median interest-bearing balance among those non-reporters is $1,900. For households with AGI of $50,000 or more the share is 53%, with a median balance of $6,200 (Johnson and Moore 2005, text and note 11). This is a within-survey comparison of asset ownership with reported income, not a link to tax records, but it points to small balances as the main source of zero answers.
- SCF 2004: only about a quarter of households under $50,000 AGI that own interest-bearing assets report any interest. The median balance for these households is $1,200 (Johnson and Moore 2008).
- Canada, about 1,500 households matched to tax returns. Among people with interest or dividends in either source, the amount appears only in the tax data for 41.4% under the paper Survey of Consumer Finances (income year 1991). The figure is 21.2% under the computer-assisted Survey of Labour and Income Dynamics test (income year 1992). By interview path, it is 13.1% for people answering from a pre-filled notebook, 8.8% for those reading from a tax form, and 36.7% for those answering unaided (Grondin and Michaud 1994, Tables 3 and 4; original read by the helper search).
- Spain, Living Conditions Survey 2007 matched to Tax Agency records by tax identification number, households with more than €100 of interest, dividends or other investment income. Of households with such income in the tax records, about 71% report none in the survey. This is computed from the paper's counts: about 2,176 of 3,057. Of households reporting such income in the survey, 16.3% have none in the tax records, partly because investment-fund income is taxed as capital gains (Méndez Martín and Vega Vicente 2011, Table 1).
- Bank-record studies of the 1950s and 1960s: known owners of savings accounts failed to report the account 19%, 22%, 24%, 26%, 35% and 46% of the time across studies (Lansing and others 1961; Ferber 1966; Ferber and others 1969, as summarized by Moore, Stinson and Welniak 2000). One Dutch study found only 5% failing to report.

**Dollar ratio.** CPS totals are 77.4% of Form 1040 interest plus dividends for ages 18–64 and 63.1% for ages 65 and older (Bee and Mitchell 2017, Table 1; Form 1040 amounts split evenly between spouses, survey values used for non-filers). The log correlation among people with both is 0.46, or 0.65 without imputed values (Appendix Table 6). This is the weakest correlation of the five income types in that table. SCF totals are 43% below SOI for taxable interest in 2000 (Johnson and Moore 2005). In the 1983 SCF they are 39% below ($95.5 billion against $157.2 billion; Avery, Elliehausen and Kennickell 1988, Table 2). In the Canadian match, the survey amount is within 5% of the tax amount for 36.4% (paper survey) and 47.4% (computer-assisted survey) of people with the income in either source. The figure is 9.5% for unaided answers, 66.3% with a pre-filled notebook and 70.3% when read from a tax form (Grondin and Michaud 1994, Tables 3 and 4). In Italy, households' reported bank deposits are 84.7% of the bank's records for the same accounts (D'Aurizio and others 2006, Table B3). An early free-recall experiment in the SIPP brought out only 22% of interest-earning savings accounts and 23% of interest-earning checking accounts before specific prompts (Moore, Marquis and Bogen 1996, secondary).

**Question wording.** When the CPS ASEC was redesigned in 2014 with separate questions for each type of asset, aggregate interest rose by 113% and dividends fell by 20% compared with the old questionnaire, in a split sample (Rothbaum 2015, presentation slides). Reported interest therefore depends heavily on how the question is asked, and a reporting rule fitted to one survey design may not carry to another.

**Other characteristics.** In the Italian bank-record study, older household heads, heads not in work and less educated heads conceal bond holdings more often. Households in the top quartile of real wealth conceal every financial asset type more often (D'Aurizio and others 2006). Norwegian respondents whose capital income exceeded a quarter of their gross income understated total income by at least 20% two-thirds of the time (Epland and Kirkeberg 2002). In the SCF, households with AGI of $50,000 or more are about twice as likely to consult tax forms during the interview (21.5% against 10.3% in 2001), and their totals line up much better with SOI (Johnson and Moore 2005). No person-level study found here models interest reporting on age, education or wealth.

## Dividends

**Who reports zero.** Dividends are combined with interest in the CPS linked study above. Bank and broker record studies found 30% of known stockholders failed to report owning stock (Ferber and others 1969b, secondary). Only 34% of stock dividends came out in free recall in the SIPP experiment (secondary).

In Italy, among bank customers who said they did not hold an asset, the bank's records show they did hold it with probability 13.6% for shares and 26.6% for mutual funds (D'Aurizio and others 2006, Table B2). This is the share of "no" answers that are wrong, not the share of true holders who say no.

**Dollar ratio.** SCF dividends are 36% below SOI in 2000 (Johnson and Moore 2005). In Italy, reported values are 54.3% of the bank's records for shares and 38.7% for mutual funds (D'Aurizio and others 2006, Table B3). In Sweden, by contrast, survey means for shares are slightly above the register and for mutual funds slightly below it (Johansson-Tormod and Klevmarken 2022, Table 3). For interest plus dividends across 1988–2003, the shortfall is 10.5–45.6% (Johnson and Moore 2008). The US record studies of stockholdings found almost no net bias in reported values among people who did report, with about 80% fully accurate (Ferber and others 1969b, secondary). The US evidence therefore points to non-reporters as the source of the shortfall. The Italian evidence finds large under-statement of amounts as well, but it measures holdings at one bank group, and some of the gap may be holdings respondents reported as held elsewhere.

**Other characteristics.** Not covered at the person level in any study found. Surveys and financial statements often label money market and bank interest "dividends", and mutual fund income is reported as dividends for tax purposes. Some interest and dividend dollars therefore move between the two lines (Coder and Scoon-Rogers 1996, an aggregate comparison).

## Retirement income (pensions, annuities and IRA distributions)

This is the best-documented item, because the CPS has been linked to the universe of Forms 1099-R.

**Who reports zero.** People 65 and older, 2013 CPS ASEC (Bee and Mitchell 2017):
- 36.6% report retirement income against 61.3% with a 1099-R. 46% of 1099-R recipients report none. False positives, meaning survey reports with no 1099-R, are rare.
- By 1099-R amount: 72% report none in the bottom fifth of amounts and 31% in the top fifth, with a monotonic decline in between (Figure 2).
- By distribution type: 40% report none when they have only employer-plan distributions, 35% when they have both kinds, and 81% when they have only IRA distributions.
- By demographics, the differences are modest. Women report none 52% of the time and men 40%. High school dropouts 56% and college graduates 40%. Ages 65–74 44%, ages 75–84 49%, ages 85 and older 50%. Disabled 44% and not disabled 47%.
- Demographics explain 6% of the variation in reporting. Adding the 1099-R features (type, amount, change from the prior year) raises this to 21% (Table 7).
- Amounts that changed by more than 10% from the prior year are reported less often.
- The ACS shows the same pattern: 44% of 1099-R recipients report none. So the pattern is not specific to the CPS questionnaire.

Survey year 2018, all ages: 54% of 1099-R recipients report no pension income (Meyer and others 2026, Appendix Table A.3). This is after the 2014 questionnaire redesign and the 2019 processing changes. False positives are 1.8%. For pensions, reporting error among respondents understates the recipient count by 36% and dollars by 38%. The understatement of dollars is about 28 points from receipt reported wrongly, mainly people reporting nothing, and about 11 points from wrong amounts among those who correctly report (Tables 3 and 4).

Bee (2013), using the 2010 CPS ASEC, all ages and unweighted: 13% of matched respondents have a 1099-R and 5% report retirement income. Two-thirds of 1099-R holders report none. Among those who report a positive amount and have a 1099-R, the log correlation is 0.77. The paper's headline that "the CPS ASEC measures retirement income well" rests on few false positives, not on reporting by true recipients. It also counts 1099-R types that the survey does not intend to cover.

**Dollar ratio among reporters.** The log correlation is 0.53 for people 65 and older, or 0.70 without imputed values (Bee and Mitchell 2017, Appendix Table 6). Under-statement among reporters accounts for 38% of the income correction at the 75th percentile of household income, but little at the bottom (Table 5). Bee (2013) finds that among reporters with five-figure 1099-R amounts, 27% under-report by a five-figure amount.

**SCF.** SCF pension and annuity income, excluding Social Security, falls far short of SOI. Combined with Social Security, it is 60% short in 2000 and 10.5–77.1% short across years, while SCF Social Security alone exceeds SOI (Johnson and Moore 2005, 2008). The authors point to IRA distributions and rollovers. In the SCF, IRA and 401(k) withdrawals are partly reported under "other income". Rollovers appear on the 1099-R and on Form 1040 line 15a but are not income. These are totals only.

## Self-employment and pass-through income

**Direction.** The survey reports more than the tax return. Among CPS ASEC respondents with positive wages plus self-employment income in both sources, those with mostly self-employment income have a mean log gap (tax minus survey) of −0.50 and a median of about −0.44. For wage earners the mean is −0.068. The regression gap between the two groups is about 40 log points (Imboden, Voorheis and Weber 2023, Figure 2 and Table 1).
- The gap is large for people with below-median administrative income. It shrinks to under 10 points above the median.
- Self-employed people bunch at the first kink of the earned income tax credit in tax data but not in survey data.
- Only 39% of the self-employed stay in the same income fifth in both sources, against 62% of wage earners.
- Restricting to people without Schedule E or F income changes the gap little. The paper is about amounts, not about who reports zero.

The Health and Retirement Study finds more self-employment income than SSA records at both the extensive and intensive margins (Abramowitz 2025, abstract only). Hurst, Li and Pugsley (2014), not a linked study, infer from spending that self-employed households under-report income to surveys by about 30%. That figure is from a search summary and was not verified.

Other countries agree on direction. In Spain, the survey amount exceeds the tax amount by about €3,400 on average for people with tax-recorded self-employment income under €9,000. The survey is about €43,800 below for people with tax-recorded self-employment income over €30,000 (Vega and Méndez 2014, among 951 people with only self-employment income in both sources). In Estonia, inferred under-reporting by the self-employed is about 56% measured against tax income and 20–25% measured against survey income (Paulus 2015). In Finland, households whose main income is self-employment or farming report about 21–33 log points less total income than the register shows (Rendtel and others 2004). The Finnish result runs the other way from the US, Spanish and Estonian findings.

**The tax return's own error.** IRS research audits put net misreporting of Schedule C income at 56–57%, farm income at 62–64%, rents and royalties at 51–53%, and partnership, S corporation, estate and trust income at 11–12% (Publication 1415, Table 5, tax years 2011–2013 and 2014–2016). Guyton and others (2023) argue that random audits miss much pass-through and offshore income at the top. In a 2003–2004 random audit of S corporations, 19.5% of net business income was found under-reported at the business level.

**SCF.** SCF business income in 2000 is more than three times the SOI figure. Capital gains and the combined rent, royalty and S corporation line are about 30% below SOI. Combining the three into one "broad business income" measure leaves the SCF 18% above SOI. The SCF often records zero where a tax return shows a loss (Johnson and Moore 2005). These are totals only.

## Capital gains

No person-level survey-to-tax study of capital gains was found. The CPS did not ask about capital gains before 2014. SCF totals for capital gains are within 30% of SOI in 2000, and about 30% below SOI when shown separately (Johnson and Moore 2005). Capital gains move between survey lines, for example being reported as business income. On the tax side, net misreporting of capital gains is 17–23% (Publication 1415). Examiners add 5.1% to reported net gains before any adjustment for missed income (Auten and Langetieg 2023).

## Social Security

**Who reports zero.** Few. People 65 and older: 85.3% report against 86.3% in SSA records, with under 8% of true recipients reporting none and roughly as many false positives (Bee and Mitchell 2017, Table 6 and Appendix Table 6). Survey year 2018, all ages: 15% of Old-Age, Survivors and Disability Insurance (OASDI) recipients report none. The figure is 14% for retirement and survivor benefits alone and 49% for disability benefits, many of which are reported as SSI instead (Meyer and others 2026, Appendix Table A.3).

**Dollar ratio.** CPS totals are 96.2% of administrative totals at 65 and older and 83.1% at ages 18–64 (Bee and Mitchell 2017, Table 1). Errors in amounts among correct reporters are near zero in net (Meyer and others 2026, Table 4: −0.1% of the target). Log correlation is 0.56, or 0.76 without imputed values. SCF Social Security is about 26% above the SOI figure in 2000 (Johnson and Moore 2005). The paper does not say whether the SOI figure is total or taxable benefits. If it is the taxable part only, the comparison is not like for like.

**Other characteristics.** The low rate of zero reports holds across most subgroups (Bee and Mitchell 2017).

## Non-US person-level studies

These were found and read by a helper search. "Read" means the helper read the full text; otherwise the level of reading is stated.

**Spain.** Méndez Martín, B., and A. Vega Vicente. 2011. "Linking data from administrative records and the Living Conditions Survey." INE Working Paper 01/2011. https://www.ine.es/GS_FILES/DocTrabajo/art_doctr012011.pdf. Person-level link, by tax identification number, of the 2007 Living Conditions Survey (the Spanish part of EU-SILC; income year 2006) to Tax Agency records. Read.
- Investment income (interest, dividends and other capital income), households above €100, 7,323 households, unweighted counts (Table 1):
  - 16.3% of survey reporters have none in the tax records.
  - 34.7% of survey non-reporters do have it.
  - About 71% of households with tax-recorded investment income report none. This share is computed from the counts.
- Amounts: the paper only says survey amounts are substantially lower.
- No breakdown by amount or characteristics.

Vega, A., and B. Méndez. 2014. INE Documento de Trabajo 2/2014. https://ine.es/GS_FILES/DocTrabajo/art_doctr022014.pdf. 2011 wave linked to income tax returns. Read.
- 9.9% of survey self-employed have no work income in the tax records. The rate is 19.0% at ages 16–24 and 18.2% at 65 and older.
- Among 951 people with only self-employment income in both sources, the means are €9,623 in the survey and €10,514 in tax records. The survey is above tax at low tax amounts and far below at high ones (see the self-employment section).

**Italy.** D'Aurizio, L., I. Faiella, S. Iezzi, and A. Neri. 2006. "L'under-reporting della ricchezza finanziaria nell'indagine sui bilanci delle famiglie." Banca d'Italia Temi di discussione 610. https://www.bancaditalia.it/pubblicazioni/temi-discussione/2006/2006-0610/tema_610.pdf. Read.
- About 1,700 customer households of one bank group were interviewed in 2003 with the Bank of Italy household survey questionnaire. Answers were matched account by account to the group's records.
- Probability of holding an asset among those who said they had none (Table B2): government bonds 6.4%, corporate and bank bonds 21.7%, shares 13.6%, mutual funds 26.6%.
- Concealment of bonds and funds rises with age: for mutual funds, from 8.5% under 30 to 35.2% over 65. It is highest in the top quartile of real wealth.
- Over-reporting of assets not held is rare. Under-reporting is correlated with the true amount.
- Reported value as a share of true value (Table B3): deposits 84.7%, government bonds 50.2%, corporate bonds 16.8%, shares 54.3%, mutual funds 38.7%.

**Canada.** Grondin, C., and S. Michaud. 1994. "Data Quality of Income Data Using Computer-Assisted Interviewing: SLID Experience." Statistics Canada SLID Research Paper 94-15. https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/75f0002m/75f0002m1994015-eng.pdf. About 1,500 households matched to tax returns. Read. Interest and dividend results are given in the interest section above. The main point is that reporting nothing falls from about 37% to about 9–13% when respondents use records.

**Norway.** Epland, J., and M. I. Kirkeberg. 2002. "Hvor stor var din inntekt i fjor?" Samfunnsspeilet 2002/6, Statistics Norway. https://www.ssb.no/a/samfunnsspeilet/utg/200206/07/. 3,166 respondents to a total-income question matched to tax returns.
- Two-thirds of those with capital income above a quarter of gross income understated total income by at least 20%. Nearly one in four pensioners understated by more than 20%, which the authors attribute to omitted capital income. These capital-income sentences were read.
- Errors are larger for the self-employed, the young, people over 70 and the less educated. High earners under-report and low earners over-report. These points come from a translated summary.

**Sweden.** Johansson-Tormod, M., and N. A. Klevmarken. 2022. "Comparing Register and Survey Wealth Data." International Journal of Microsimulation 15(1): 43–62. https://www.microsimulation.pub/articles/00249. SHARE-Sweden 2003 matched to register asset values. Read for Table 3 and the net worth result.
- Survey and register means for the same respondents are close for bank holdings (130,524 against 130,198 kronor) and within about 25% for bonds, shares and mutual funds, in both directions.
- No false-negative shares.
- Survey errors are reportedly negatively correlated with register values. This was not checked.

Kapteyn, A., and J. Y. Ypma. 2007. "Measurement Error and Misclassification: A Comparison of Survey and Administrative Data." Journal of Labor Economics 25(3): 513–551. Earnings of people aged 50 and older only; no capital income. Abstract and secondary sources only. Its point for us is that allowing for errors in the register and in the linkage removes much of the apparent mean reversion in survey earnings.

**Denmark and Finland, total income only.**
- Kreiner, Lassen and Leth-Petersen (2015, NBER volume 74, https://www.nber.org/chapters/c12663.pdf) find a slope of 0.57 (0.84 after trimming) from regressing register income on survey-reported total income. Capital income is not reported separately.
- Bingley and Martinello (2014, SHARE Working Paper 16-2014) find no mean bias in total household income in SHARE-Denmark, with 36% of the variance being error.
- Finnish European Community Household Panel data (Nordberg 2003; Rendtel and others 2004, CHINTEX working papers 15 and 21):
  - Survey household income is 5–8% below the register on average.
  - It is 17–20% below in the top register decile and 12–14% above in the bottom decile.
  - Having any capital income is associated with slightly less under-reporting, not more.

**Austria, wages, pensions and benefits.**
- Angel, Disslbacher, Humer and Schnetzer (2019, JRSS-A 182(4)), Austrian EU-SILC 2008–2011 matched to registers: low-decile respondents over-report and top-decile respondents under-report wages and pensions. Read through a summary only.
- Bollinger and Tasseva (2023, Public Opinion Quarterly 87(S1)), same data: 8.9% of register earners report zero earnings, and 38–52% of unemployment benefit recipients report zero. Much of the latter is confusion between benefit types. Read through a summary only.

**New Zealand and Estonia, self-employment.**
- Cabral, Gemmell and Alinaghi (2021, International Tax and Public Finance 28(2)) report that tax-based estimates of self-employed under-reporting are about double the survey-based ones. Abstract only.
- Paulus (2015, ISER Working Paper 2015-15) is described in the self-employment section.

Across countries, the pattern for capital income matches the US. A large share of register recipients report nothing: 71% for investment income above €100 in Spain, and about 21–41% for interest and dividends in Canada depending on the survey design. Using records sharply reduces the share. The Italian study adds that concealment rises with age and wealth.

## The tax return's own errors (IRS research audits)

The IRS's main measure is the net misreporting percentage. For one line, it is under-statements minus over-statements, divided by the sum of the absolute values of the correct amounts. Unless stated, the figures include the IRS's statistical estimate of income that examiners did not find.

| Line item | Tax years 2011–2013 | Tax years 2014–2016 |
|---|---|---|
| Wages, salaries, tips | 1% | 1% |
| Taxable interest | 1% | 4% |
| Dividends | 5% | 4% |
| Pensions and annuities | 3% | 4% |
| Taxable Social Security benefits | 11% | 13% |
| Capital gains | 23% | 18% |
| Partnership, S corporation, estate and trust income | 11% | 12% |
| Schedule C (nonfarm sole proprietor) | 56% | 57% |
| Farm income | 62% | 64% |
| Rents and royalties | 51% | 53% |

Source: IRS Publication 1415 (Rev. 9-2019 and Rev. 10-2022), Table 5. Read in full by the helper search. Later IRS publications (5869, 2023 and 2024) are projections that reuse the 2014–2016 rates.

Without the adjustment for missed income, examiners add 0.2% to wages, 2.1% to interest plus dividends, 2.3% to retirement income, 4.0% to taxable Social Security, 5.1% to net capital gains, 5.7% to partnership and S corporation income and 54.2% to Schedule C (Auten and Langetieg 2023, Table 1, averaged over 1988, 2001 and 2006–2013). Ranked by true income, net misreported income is 8–10% of true income for every class below $1 million and 4% above it. The IRS warns that the top figure may miss pass-through, offshore and digital-asset income (IRS Research, Applied Analytics and Statistics 2024, Table 2.4).

For interest, dividends and retirement distributions, the information return (1099-INT, 1099-DIV, 1099-R) and the tax return agree to within a few percent of dollars. For the rebuild this means that, for these items, the PUF amount can be treated as close to the truth, and differences between the survey and the PUF are mostly survey error. For Schedule C, farm and rental income, the PUF amount is itself well below the truth, and a survey may be closer to it.

## What this suggests as priors for our reporting rules

These are hedged statements. Most rest on the CPS, a few on the SCF compared with tax totals, and none on an SCF person-level link.

1. **Model reporting nothing as a separate step from the amount.** For interest, dividends and retirement distributions, most of the survey shortfall comes from true recipients reporting zero. Under-statement by people who do report is a smaller part. The CPS evidence on pensions puts reporting error on amounts at roughly a quarter of the dollar shortfall (about 11 points of a 38-point understatement in 2017).
2. **The chance of reporting zero should fall with the true amount, but not to zero.** For 1099-R income among people 65 and older, the rate of reporting nothing goes from 72% in the bottom fifth of amounts to 31% in the top fifth. For interest, the SCF non-reporters hold median interest-bearing balances of $1,200–$6,200, At a 2% interest rate, for example, $1,200 earns $24 a year and $6,200 earns $124. A reasonable starting prior is a reporting probability that rises steeply in log true amount for small amounts and levels off below one. On the order of 25–35% of large recipients would still report zero for retirement income, and probably fewer for interest. No study gives the interest curve directly.
3. **Among people who report, the reported amount should be centered near the true amount with wide noise.** Log correlations between survey and administrative amounts among people with both are about 0.5–0.7 for interest plus dividends and for retirement income, and 0.7–0.85 for wages, with the higher figures when imputed survey values are excluded. Net bias in amounts is small for wages: within about 4–7% of the tax amount, with the sign differing between studies. It is moderate for pensions.
4. **Few false positives for capital-income items.** A survey report of interest, dividends or pensions where tax records show none is rare: under 2% of non-recipients for pensions. A small false-positive rate is still needed. Some of it is real, because amounts below the $10 information-return threshold are not on a 1099, and some of it is misclassification between interest, dividends and business income.
5. **Wages and Social Security: near one-to-one.** Reporting zero is rare (Social Security under 8–15%, apart from disability benefits). Survey totals come to 96–100% of administrative totals.
6. **Self-employment: the survey amount is larger than the tax amount.** The prior should allow the survey to exceed the tax return. A gap of about 40 log points above the wage-earner gap is a reasonable center, concentrated among people with low tax-return income. The tax return is not truth here (net misreporting on Schedule C is about 56%).
7. **Retirement distributions: separate IRA withdrawals from employer pensions if the data allow.** IRA-only recipients report nothing about 80% of the time, against 40% for employer-plan recipients. The SCF also puts some IRA withdrawals under "other income", and 1099-R totals include rollovers that are not income.
8. **Characteristics other than the amount matter, but less.** In the Italian bank study, concealing financial assets rises with age and wealth. In the CPS, sex and education move the zero-report rate for retirement income by about 12–16 percentage points. Age within 65 and older moves it by about 6 points. Demographics explain about 6% of the variation, against 21% once the type and size of the 1099-R are included. In the SCF, reporting is better for households with AGI of $50,000 or more and for those who consult tax forms. Our priors should therefore put most of the structure on the true amount and type, and allow weaker effects for age, sex, education and income.
9. **Question design matters.** The 2014 CPS redesign roughly doubled reported interest. A reporting rule should be specific to a survey and its questionnaire version, not shared across surveys by default.
10. **The SCF probably under-reports interest and dividends less than the CPS.** It oversamples high-wealth households, asks detailed asset questions, and many high-income respondents consult tax forms. Its interest total is close to the IRS total even though its recipient share (about 13%) is about half the tax-return share (about 26%). This is consistent with the CPS pattern: small holders report zero, large holders report near the truth. It is an inference from totals, not a measured person-level result.

## Gaps: what no study found here covers

- **No published person-level SCF-to-IRS comparison of reported income items.** The Federal Reserve matches list-sample cases to the SOI Individual Tax File for sample design (Kennickell 1999 used 1,519 matched 1995 records). Kennickell writes that legal and ethical limits restrict any such match to the list sample and to narrow uses. If the rebuild wants SCF-specific reporting rates, a request to the SCF team for tabulations from the list-sample match is the only route seen.
- **No published zero-reporting rates by amount for interest or dividends separately in the CPS.** The Census Bureau holds 1099-INT and 1099-DIV amounts linked to the CPS (Rothbaum 2023, cited in the NEWS working paper as unpublished). It has released only imputation coefficients for non-filers.
- **No person-level evidence on capital gains, partnership and S corporation income, or rents and royalties** in any US survey.
- **No evidence for the area-probability part of the SCF versus the list part,** or for very high-income respondents. The CPS evidence is mostly for typical households.
- **Household versus person.** The CPS studies split joint-return interest and dividends evenly between spouses. The SCF reports at the level of the primary economic unit. Neither matches the tax-unit concept exactly.
- **The administrative side is not perfect.** Linkage errors (Kapteyn and Ypma 2007) and non-filers' missing 1040 data affect every linked study. The CPS interest and dividend comparison uses survey values for non-filers.
- **Several cited figures are secondary.** These are Coder (1992) and the bank-record studies, both via Moore, Stinson and Welniak (2000), plus Abowd and Stinson (2013), Abramowitz (2025), Hurst, Li and Pugsley (2014) and Johns and Slemrod (2010). They should be checked against the originals before any number from them enters a model.
